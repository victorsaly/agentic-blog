# Every agent takes something away

Every time you build something new, it takes something away. Decide what, before you build it.

## Start with something real

A company brings in a new agent. Agents are the new thing, everywhere. This one handles a lot of workflow that used to be manual.

It works brilliantly. It answers customers at 3 a.m., in different languages. Customers are happy. The company saves money. The team can focus on other things. Everyone wins.

## Two years later

Someone realises that nobody in the company really understands the product any more.

People used to become experts by reading the tickets, answering them and solving the problems. They learned from the mistakes. That learning curve went to the agent.

When nobody understands what is happening, nobody decides what to do. The whole workflow is in jeopardy, and nobody can see what was taken away.

I think about this a lot. It bothers me more since I started building more agents and giving them responsibility for things people used to do.

## The wrong question

I think we ask the wrong questions.

When something goes wrong with technology, one sentence always comes out: technology is neither good nor bad, it depends what you use it for. It sounds reasonable. I think it is a convenient excuse.

It means nobody takes responsibility. Not the people who built it, not the people who sold it, not the people who decided to use it.

When you build an agent, you are not just making a tool. You are deciding what people are used for. You are deciding what it will do for them, and, more important, what it will stop them doing.

Those decisions are much older than AI. They are older than computers. So let me take a detour.

## A cup, and Aristotle

A cup is a technology. Why does it exist? The obvious answer is that somebody made it.

Two thousand years ago, Aristotle described four causes behind everything that exists: what it is made of, what form it takes, who made it, and what it is for. The cup is ceramic, it has a shape, someone made it, and its purpose is to drink.

Obvious, yes. But look at the order. Someone has to imagine drinking before they design the cup. The purpose comes first. The object is that purpose made solid.

We talk about technology as if it were neutral. Even a cup is a decision about what matters. It is the same with software, and it is true with agents. Every agent starts with someone deciding what they want it to do.

## Heidegger and people as resources

Heidegger looked at how technology changes the relationship between people and the world.

Think about a farmer, in the old way. The farmer works with nature. He understands the soil, the seasons, what the land can give. Now think about an industrial power station. The river is no longer really a river. It is a source of energy. Everything is measured by what it can deliver, and we lose something we used to have.

Heidegger called this way of seeing the world "enframing". Everything is seen through the lens of what it can do for us. He had a name for the result too: "standing reserve", where everything becomes a resource waiting to be used.

This is where it gets close to our world. What happens when we look at people the same way? A customer becomes a conversion. An employee becomes a capacity. A user's attention becomes engagement. We already see this language in dashboards, reports and business systems.

An agent will happily optimise whatever we tell it to optimise. If we measure people as resources, the agent will treat them as resources. That is not a problem of the AI model. It is a problem in how we design the system in the first place.

Technology does not only change what we can do. It changes what we see, including how we see each other.

## What disappears

Take everyday things. The car lets you travel further and faster, and it took away a lot of walking. The phone lets you reach almost anyone, almost anywhere, and it took away the ability to be unreachable. The word processor made documents much easier, and it changed the skills people need to work in an office.

We talk endlessly about what new technology gives us. It goes in the launch announcement, the marketing campaign, the product demo. We rarely spend the same time on what disappears.

In the study of technology there is a concept called affordances: the possibilities for action that a technology creates. When we design agents, we should pay equal attention to the other side. What are we removing? What are we no longer practising? What are we handing over, so that we eventually forget how to do it?

Foucault had an interesting view on power. He was suspicious of systems that exercise power while presenting themselves as neutral. I think that is worth remembering. An agent that is "simply making things easier" may also be making certain choices for us.

So perhaps we should not only ask whether the tool works. We should also ask what happened to the people using it. The feature is half of the story. We need to write the other half.

## Three agents

Back to the customer support example. I think three kinds of agent make this obvious.

**The support agent.** It answers tickets automatically and closes the issues. It works around the clock, so response is faster, costs are lower, and it can support many languages. What it takes away is the apprentice: the chance for someone new to learn the product by dealing with real customer problems.

There is a design decision hiding here. Does the agent close the ticket completely? Or does it prepare the answer, so a human reviewer learns from it and takes responsibility? That is how we decide how much we take away, and how much we leave for a human to decide and learn from.

**The meeting agent.** We all know this one. It joins the meeting, records everything and produces a nice summary. Nobody needs to take notes. People who could not attend can catch up. Brilliant.

But what else are we giving away? The reason to listen properly in the first place.

Simone Weil wrote a lot about attention. She believed that paying attention was one of the most important things we can do. Listening is not only collecting information. It is noticing things, questioning them, understanding why someone disagrees, sometimes changing your mind. A summary gives you the information, but not the experience that led to it.

So here is a design question. Should the agent simply summarise the meeting? Or should it point to the biggest disagreements, or the questions that still need human judgement? Those are two different products.

**The recommendation agent.** This one is less noticeable. It learns what you like and starts choosing what you should watch, read, listen to or buy next. It gets very good at predicting what you will enjoy. It is convenient, personal, and saves searching.

But think about the most interesting things we experience. A book someone put in your hands. Music you found by accident. An idea you disagreed with that still changed what you think. A recommendation system can narrow your world without you noticing.

Steven Case (the name is unclear in my recording) says freedom is not only being able to choose. It also depends on having options to choose from, and being able to consider them. These agents quietly decide which options you get to see. They have influenced your freedom before you make a decision.

Again there are architecture questions. How many options should the agent show? Should it sometimes recommend something completely outside your usual interest? This is not only a technical question. It is about what kind of experience we want people to have.

## Write down what it takes

None of this means we should not build these agents. It means we should understand what we are building beyond the feature list.

For every feature, write down what it gives and what it takes away. If you cannot, perhaps you do not understand the feature yet. We need to know what we are taking and what we are giving.

## Not against technology

I do not want this to become an argument against technology. That would be only half of the story.
